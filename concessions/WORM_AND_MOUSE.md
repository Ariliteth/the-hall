# WORM AND MOUSE
### A Score for Fixed Point Local

---

A metal worm and a nerdy mouse are raiding office filing cabinets for tradeable secrets. They start from the ground level and work their way up. The player is not them. The player is the dispatcher — the person behind the screen who smirks and says *we're in.*

Few plans survive contact intact. That's expected. That's the job.

---

## What The Player Sees

Two surfaces, always visible simultaneously.

**The Blueprint** occupies most of the screen. It is a schematic — slightly wrong, slightly old. A photocopied floor plan of the current cabinet level. Faint grid. Thick outline strokes. General shapes for walls and zones, nothing exact. This is what the dispatcher has. It is not the room.

**The Live Signal** burns through the blueprint as sparse readable marks. Agent blips move in something close to real time. Mouse is a small precise dot. Worm is a slow line that leaves trace — thin, persisting, fading gradually. The trace is the only part of the map that updates without dispatcher input.

These two layers share one screen. The blueprint is what we know. The signal is what's happening. The gap between them is the game.

Below the blueprint: two agent queue strips, one per operative. Below those: the log thread.

---

## The Agents

### Mouse

Nerdy, planned, discerning. Knows more than she says. Reports precisely.

Mouse plans ahead. Her queue holds five to seven action chips at a time. She fills it back in when she sees a new prize or resolves her current path — not constantly, only when she has reason to look forward again. She is the leftmost operative. Her blip on the blueprint is small and moves with intention.

Mouse's communication in the log is clipped first-person. *That wall is paper, not metal — adjusting.* *Repeat, HQ? Objective marker puts me in the open.* *She's not passive.* *Further out, boss.*

Mouse is comforted by Worm's proximity. When they are adjacent her actions have slightly less variance. She can climb Worm's body as traversal — Worm's body is infrastructure.

### Worm

Metal. Grows as it burrows. Moves fast, reports rarely, arrives places. Not flustered by walls — walls are just the medium.

Worm's queue holds one to three action chips and refreshes constantly. He does not plan. He responds to what is immediately in front of him. His blip on the blueprint is the head of a thin line — the line being his accumulated trace.

Worm's communication in the log is short and physical. *through.* *metal here.* *found something.* *mouse nearby, good.*

Worm grows longer each time he burrows. This is not a reward. This is just what happens.

---

## The Queue Strips

Each agent has a visible queue strip below the blueprint. These are not control surfaces. They are visibility surfaces — the dispatcher seeing what the agents intend.

**Chips** are small physical-feeling objects. Colored. Each color corresponds to an action type. An additional letter or small emoji on the chip is the subtag — what the chip knows about its own intent. A green chip is SLIDE. A green chip with ❄️ means she's planning a long cold-surface slide and knows it. A red chip with 💡 means Worm has a specific target in mind.

Chips are not issued by the dispatcher. Agents build their own queues based on what they see and what they want. The dispatcher watches.

**Tapping a chip** is asking *are you sure?* The agent re-evaluates. If their target is still valid they confirm and the chip executes. If they're uncertain the chip reshapes — becomes something adjacent, improvised. Mouse responds to taps more than Worm does. Worm is already committed.

**Convo bubbles** surface above chips when an agent wants to flag something. Mouse's bubbles are words and a specific emoji. Worm's are mostly just an emoji and sometimes a direction. A 💡 bubble means she thinks she knows where something is and wants confirmation. An 💥 bubble means something changed and she's adapting. These are not alerts. They are the agents talking to us.

**Mouse's queue** is long, deliberate, ordered. Its length communicates her confidence.

**Worm's queue** is short, refreshing constantly. Its brevity communicates his nature.

---

## Pacing

Agents act on their own rhythm. Worm's rhythm is fast. Mouse's is slower and considered. The dispatcher is not keeping pace with turns — the dispatcher is present, watching, occasionally doing something.

Most of the time nothing is required of the dispatcher. The log updates. The blips move. The chips cycle.

**Pause-for-witness moments** — moments where everything stops and waits for the dispatcher to register what happened — are rare and earned. They include:

- A TRUST ME sticker being tested for the first time
- A gnome resolving from edge disturbance into confirmed presence
- A floor key found
- An agent entering full improvisation because the dispatcher's target wasn't there

Outside these moments, things keep moving. The dispatcher is not the one setting the tempo.

## The Dispatcher's Tools

### Stickers

Placed on the blueprint. Small, physical, draggable. These are transmissions — every sticker placed is something sent to the agents, whether or not we intend it as communication.

Stickers have two precision states. A quick tap-place is approximate: *roughly here, use your judgment.* A held press is confident: *exactly here, I'm certain.* Agents read the difference. Mouse in particular.

Stickers placed during a run cost a **gnomaction** — a moment in which gnomes may move, rotate, or respond to the pressure of the dispatcher's decision before the action is committed. This is not a penalty screen. It is a consequence that happens in the world.

**OBJECTIVE** — we think something important is here. Agents will path toward it when their queue empties or they're choosing between options. Agent feedback on placement:
- Objective near confirmed position: *On it.*
- Objective somewhere unverified: *Repeat, HQ?*
- Objective clearly wrong: *Further out, boss.*

**CAUTION** — something feels wrong in this zone. Agents weight careful actions when near it. Mouse may shorten her queue. Worm probably ignores it.

**WALL** — we think there's solid structure here. Helps agents anticipate. Wrong wall placements land in the dispatcher's INT monologue: *that wasn't load-bearing then.*

**CLEAR** — we believe this zone is open passage. Agents use this for routing confirmation.

**TRUST ME** — no explanation attached. The agent responds based on accumulated relationship across runs. Early runs: ignored or questioned. Later runs: Mouse reroutes without asking why. Worm still ignores it but moves slightly more carefully than usual. The relationship is the mechanic.

**CONTACT** — we sense something incoming from a direction. One or two available per floor. Placed near an edge of the blueprint. Does not specify what or who. Just pressure. Mouse checks her queue when one lands. Worm doesn't respond, but may get surprised by what the contact turned out to be.

### Contextual Pins

The sidebar manifest. Contains only what the dispatcher has intelligence on for this cabinet. One file pin if we know there's one file. A key pin placed where the brief said it probably is. Running out of a pin means we've committed all our knowledge of that thing.

Pins can be moved after placement if we learn better. Agents respond to moved pins if they had already acknowledged the original position.

## Gnomes: Cabignomes

Gnomes exist in the physical layer. The agents share space with them. The dispatcher shares a different kind of space with them — dimensional, oblique.

On the blueprint, gnomes do not appear as blips. They appear as **edge disturbance** — a smear, a shadow at the margin of the schematic — before they are fully inside the floor. This is how the dispatcher perceives them: as pressure before presence. The CONTACT sticker is the dispatcher's response to this.

Once a gnome is inside the floor and an agent has confirmed eyes on it, it resolves into a small chip on that agent's queue — a gnome icon with a disposition read. Passive gnomes render differently from alerted ones. The dispatcher never sees this directly. The dispatcher infers it from agent behavior and log reports.

**Gnomes have opinions about the decision dimension.** Some rotate when the dispatcher places a sticker. Some twitch when a queue chip is tapped. A gnome near a freshly placed CONTACT pin might look toward the pin location before looking away. They sense planning as pressure. They don't understand it. They feel it.

The gnome that rotates on a sticker placement and the gnome that twitches on a queue tap are probably different gnomes. This is not stated anywhere. It is observed or not.

**Disposition is not labeled.** It reveals itself through behavior. *She's not passive* is Mouse telling the dispatcher something she had to discover. The dispatcher notes it. There is no gnome icon added to the sidebar. We just know now.

Gnomes move independently of agent turns. They have their own rhythm, which is neither synchronized with the dispatcher nor with the agents. They mill. Occasionally they do something that is not milling.

## Tile Reactivity

The agents experience the cabinet. The dispatcher does not. Tile identity is revealed through agent behavior, not through the blueprint.

Mouse sliding two tiles instead of one tells the dispatcher that tile was metal. The blueprint does not update automatically. The dispatcher updates their understanding by adjusting stickers.

**Metal** — Mouse slides, moving two tiles instead of one from a metal-origin. Not always unwelcome. Worm moves faster on metal. Metal tiles remember worm passage and become pre-softened — easier to re-burrow on a return.

**Paper** — tears on impact. Worm tears through and leaves ragged passage. Debris can impede gnome movement. Mouse pushes through slowly.

**Cardboard** — compresses. Mouse can push cardboard blocks, changing room geometry. A pushed block is new cover, new obstacle, new information. The dispatcher sees the effect, not the cause.

**Wood** — splinters. Loud. Gnomes react to splinter events even without line of sight to the cause.

**Cabinet hardware** (drawers, handles, rails) — climbable by Mouse. Traversable by Worm if it has grown long enough to bridge the gap. Keys are often on or near hardware.

## The Planning Phase

Before agents enter a floor, the dispatcher gets the blueprint alone. No blips. No trace. No gnome weather. Just the schematic and the manifest.

This is mission prep. The dispatcher places initial pins and stickers. Where we think the key is. Where we think the file is. Best guesses at wall positions in tricky zones. A TRUST ME if there's a feeling.

The planning phase ends with a single action that sends the agents in.

The planning phase returns between floors. Agents wait at the threshold while the dispatcher reviews the next schematic with what was learned. Pins can be moved. Stickers can be updated. A CONTACT can be placed at an edge if the last floor said something about how gnomes enter.

---

## The Log Thread

The shared voice channel. Runs along the bottom of the screen. Two registers, interleaved.

**Agent reports** — clipped, first-person, specific. Present tense. What is happening in the room.

**Dispatcher INT** — smaller, different typographic weight, offset. The dispatcher talking to the screen. Not addressed to agents. Not commands. The sound of someone trying to keep up. *That wasn't in the schematic.* *She knows something I don't.* *Worm is already three tiles past the marker.* *Okay.*

When a sticker or pin is placed mid-run, the relevant agent may respond in the log. This response appears in the agent register, not the INT register.

The log is not a status feed. It is a conversation that the dispatcher is partially participating in and partially overhearing.

---

## Progression

**Within a cabinet** — three floors. Each floor the schematic is slightly more useful because the dispatcher has learned how this cabinet is built. This knowledge doesn't transfer automatically. It lives in what the dispatcher chooses to place and where.

**Across runs** — the trust relationship with agents accumulates. TRUST ME stickers that proved correct are remembered. Mouse's response to them shifts over time. Worm's doesn't, but the dispatcher learns to work with that.

The manifest expands slightly as runs progress. A second CONTACT pin might unlock. A contextual pin might appear if extracted intel revealed something about the floor above.

---

## Visual Register

Institutional. Worn. The kind of office that was probably nice once.

Photocopied schematic on aged paper. Mimeograph texture. The palette is mold green, ink black, paper cream, stamp red, aged brass. Filing cabinet gray. The chips on the queue strips are the brightest color on screen — small, readable, physical.

The dispatcher's INT monologue sits small and apart. One line at a time. Updating. It is not a subtitle. It is a terminal.

---

## What This Isn't

Not a tactics game. The dispatcher is not optimizing.

Not a puzzle. The room does not have a correct solution.

Not a story the dispatcher is authoring. The agents have their own reasons. The worm was already through the wall.

## Implementation Notes for Code

**State split** — the blueprint layer and the live signal layer share a screen but are rendered separately. The blueprint is dispatcher-authored state. The live signal is agent-world state. They should not be the same data structure even if they overlap visually.

**Agent queues** are agent-owned. The dispatcher reads them. Tapping a chip sends a query, not a command — the agent's next tick resolves the query and reshapes or confirms the chip.

**Gnomaction cost** — sticker placement during a live run should trigger a gnome tick before the sticker resolves visually. The gnome moves, then the sticker lands. This sequence is the cost.

**Pacing** — agents run on independent timers. Mouse's timer is slower. Worm's is faster. The dispatcher has no timer. Pause-for-witness moments freeze all timers until the dispatcher acknowledges.

**Trust accumulation** — TRUST ME outcomes should be stored per-run and summarized into a running score that modifies Mouse's response threshold. This does not need to be surfaced to the player as a number.

**Tile identity** — tiles have types that are known to the agent-world state but not to the blueprint state. The blueprint state updates only when an agent action explicitly confirms a tile (by behavior or log report). Wrong sticker placements are not errors — they are information the dispatcher didn't have.

**The log** — two message types, rendered differently. Agent messages and INT messages should be easily distinguishable by weight and position without being labeled as such.
